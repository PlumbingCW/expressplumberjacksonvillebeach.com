Humming, jammed or dead: fix the symptom before you pay for a new unit

A disposal has one moving job, a flywheel with impellers driven by a motor. When it hums and stalls, something is jamming the flywheel, such as a bone, a fruit pit or a bottle cap. Unplugging the unit or switching off the breaker, then turning the flywheel with an Allen key from underneath, frees many jams.

If the unit is silent, check the red reset button on the bottom and the circuit breaker. A tripped reset is the motor's overload protection, and one that trips repeatedly means a worn motor or a recurring jam. That is when a plumber should look at it.

Disposal leaking right now? Top, side and bottom leaks

A disposal leak ruins a cabinet floor quietly. Water from the top flange or the drain connection can be fixed with a new seal or a tightened ring. Water from the bottom or the body means the housing or an internal seal has failed, and the unit has to be replaced.

The dishwasher connects to the side of most disposals through a short hose, and that spot leaks often. We check the hose, the clamp and the knockout plug that must be removed on a new unit. If the dishwasher drains slowly after a new disposal, a plug that was never knocked out is the usual cause.

Repair or replace: how we decide

A jam or a loose connection is a repair. A leaking body or a motor that overloads or will not restart is a replacement. We tell you which side you are on once we have the unit apart, and give you the price before we start. Horsepower, noise insulation and feed type (continuous or batch) are the choices on a new unit.

What we check on the way out

  • The trap and drain line past the disposal for hidden clogs.
  • The dishwasher hose and its high loop connection.
  • The wall switch and power cord or hard-wire connection.
  • The under-sink supply lines and shutoff valves, which often need attention at the same visit.
  • Whether a drain cleaning visit is needed because the disposal was masking a slow line.

Kitchens, grease and the city's FOG program

Why grease matters even in a home kitchen

Jacksonville Beach operates a Grease Interceptor (FOG) Program under Ordinances 2019-8125 and 2020-8156, with a rebate program. That program targets restaurants and food businesses, and our commercial plumbing page covers that side. For a home kitchen, the lesson is practical: fats, oils and grease poured down the disposal cool and coat the drain line.

A disposal grinds food, it does not make grease safe for the pipes. Scrape pans, wipe oil into the trash, and run cold water with the disposal for a few seconds after use. A drain that backs up even after the disposal is cleared points to a line problem, and a camera check or hydro jetting may be the answer.

A disposal drain connects to the sink trap under Florida's plumbing code, which takes its model language from the IPC, and the electrical side follows the electrical code. If the unit is hard wired, we leave the circuit work to a licensed electrician.
