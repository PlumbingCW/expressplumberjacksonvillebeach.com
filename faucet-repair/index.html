Start under the sink: the shutoff valves

Every sink, toilet and many appliances should have a small shutoff valve on the supply line, usually called an angle stop. Closing it stops water to that fixture only, so you keep the rest of the house running. Turn it clockwise until it stops. If the handle will not move, do not force it, because an old valve can snap and turn a drip into a flood.

A stuck or leaking shutoff valve is one of the most common calls we get, and it is often the reason a simple faucet repair becomes an emergency. Valves that have not been turned in years can seize or weep around the stem. Replacing them while we are there is cheap insurance, and we point out any that look tired.

A supply line has burst and water will not stop

If water is spraying from under a sink, the cause is usually a cracked braided supply line, a failed compression nut, or a seized or broken shutoff. Close the main valve at the meter or where the line enters the house. Then open a faucet at the lowest point to drain pressure. We can replace supply lines and valves on the same visit, including after-hours emergency calls.

Salt air, metal finishes and what it does to fixtures

Jacksonville Beach sits right on the Atlantic, and coastal air carries salt that attacks exposed metal over time. Chrome and brushed finishes can pit, handles can stiffen, and the small metal parts under the sink corrode faster than in an inland house. That is general coastal wear, not something we can put a number on.

What it means for you: do not assume a stiff handle is a simple cartridge problem. Look under the sink for green or white crust on the shutoff valve and supply nuts. If you see it, replace the valve along with the faucet repair. Outdoor spigots and fixtures near the ocean side of the house tend to wear first, so check those before a hose bib becomes a spray.

Repair or replace the faucet

Many drips are a worn cartridge, washer or O-ring, and a repair costs far less than a new fixture. Replace the faucet when the body is corroded or pitted, the finish is flaking, parts are no longer made, or the faucet is old enough that repairs keep recurring.

Showers, tubs and outdoor spigots

Shower valves and tub faucets follow the same logic. A drip from the showerhead after you turn it off points to a worn cartridge inside the wall valve, and a tub spout that runs when the shower is on is usually a diverter problem. Replacing a cartridge is normally a faceplate-off job and does not require opening the wall.

A shower or tub that drains slowly is often a hair clog in the trap, and a snake or drain cleaning clears it. If several fixtures back up together, the problem is deeper in the line, and our drain cleaning team can take over. An outdoor hose bib that leaks around the handle or inside the wall may need a new valve or a freeze-protected replacement.

Faucets sold for home use are built to meet national plumbing product standards, and the IPC based Florida Building Code, Plumbing governs how they are connected. A shutoff that will not close is the usual reason a simple faucet job turns into a valve job.
