Signs the pipes in your walls are done

Most repipes start with a pattern, not one leak. You patch a pinhole under the sink, then another shows up in the hall closet a few months later. Each repair holds, but the pipe around it is thinning from the inside, and the next failure is usually close by.

Patterns that point to a repipe

  • Pinhole leaks that keep appearing in different rooms, often with green or white crust on the pipe
  • Brown or rust-tinted water when a tap has sat unused, especially from older steel lines
  • Water pressure that has faded fixture by fixture, even though the city side is fine
  • Stains on drywall or ceilings that dry out and come back
  • Pipes that have been repaired several times with clamps, couplings or push-fit patches

Galvanized steel is the usual culprit in older houses. It corrodes from the inside, so the opening narrows until a shower runs weak even though the line looks fine from outside. Jacksonville Beach was settled in 1884 and has a large stock of older homes, so galvanized and aging copper runs are still out there behind plaster and paneling.

Repipe or repair: how we decide

A repipe costs more than a single repair, so we start by finding out whether the problem is one bad stretch or the whole system. We check the pipe material, look at fittings where we can reach them, and test pressure with the house shut off. If one run is bad and the rest is sound, we say so and fix only that run.

If the leaks are spreading, a leak hunt first is smart. Our leak detection service confirms where water is escaping before anyone cuts drywall.

A word on city water and your pipes

The city's 2025 water quality report lists lead and copper results with zero sites above the action level. That tells you about the water leaving the city system, not about the pipe inside your walls. If your house has old steel or soldered joints, the plumbing in the building is a separate question, and you can ask the Water Division for the annual report if you want the city's numbers.

PEX or copper: choosing the material

Both materials are accepted for house repipes, and either one is a big step up from corroded steel. The better pick depends on your walls, your budget and how you feel about each material. We will tell you which one we would put in your house and why.

How a repipe works in a house people live in

We schedule the work so you are not without water for long. The new system is run alongside the old one where possible, then tied in and tested before we cut over. Expect a short shutoff for the cutover, and we tell you the timing before we start.

Access holes go where they are easiest to patch, often behind fixtures and in closets. We protect floors and clean up daily, and we pressure test every new line before you use it. Patching and paint are separate trades, so we talk through that handoff at the walkthrough.

Repiping is regulated work. It is done to the Florida Building Code, Plumbing, drawn from the International Plumbing Code (IPC), usually needs a permit and inspection, and must be performed under a properly licensed contractor as required by Chapter 489, Florida Statutes.

Permits and inspections for a repipe in Jacksonville Beach

The City of Jacksonville Beach uses a plumbing e-permit through its Building Inspection Division, part of Planning and Development. The application asks you to classify the work, and "re-pipe" is one of the listed classifications, alongside alteration, new, repair and fuel gas. It also asks whether your water source is city or well.

The city states that work may not begin until a permit is approved and posted on site, and its plans follow the Florida Building Code 2023 edition. We pull the permit for jobs we do, so you do not have to learn the form. If you ever want to confirm what applies to your project, the Building Inspection Division at 904-247-6100 can answer.

Low pressure, copper repairs and other jobs that lead to a repipe

Low water pressure is the symptom people notice first. Before recommending a repipe we check the simple things: a partly closed valve, a clogged aerator, a failing pressure regulator. If the pressure drops only at certain fixtures, or the water runs brown at first, the pipe is the likely cause.

Copper repairs are a different story. A single failed joint or a freeze-damaged section can often be cut out and replaced with new copper, which saves a repipe. If a run has multiple failures, we will explain when a repair stops making sense. For a cracked line that needs attention today, our burst pipe repair team can stop the water first.

What drives the price of a repipe

Every house is different, so we do not quote by square footage on the phone. These are the things that move the price, and we go through each one at the walkthrough.

Size and layoutMore fixtures and more floors mean more pipe and more access work. A single-story house with a central bathroom block is simpler than a two-story with several wet walls.
Pipe materialPEX and copper are priced differently, and the fittings and labor differ as well. We show both when both make sense.
Wall and attic accessOpen framing and a clear attic make the job faster. Plaster, tile and tight chases take longer to reach.
Fixture shutoffs and connectionsNew stop valves at sinks and toilets are worth doing during a repipe, and they are part of the plan we price.
Permit and testingThe permit, inspection scheduling and pressure testing are part of the job, not an add-on.

Homeowner policies usually cover sudden and accidental water damage, but not wear, gradual leaks or the failed pipe itself. A repipe is normally a maintenance expense. If a pipe has burst, photograph the damage and check your own policy before repairs begin.
