Why slabs and low ground matter on the beaches

Most homes in Florida are built slab-on-grade, which means the house sits on a concrete pad poured at ground level and the plumbing is cast in or buried under it. Jacksonville Beach sits on a barrier island, with land at or near sea level and roughly two-thirds of the city area made up of water. Your home is very likely on a slab, though an older or raised house may differ, so confirm what you have.

Low ground means the soil beneath a slab is often damp on its own. That cuts two ways. A cool, damp patch at the slab edge is not proof of a leak, which is why we confirm with listening and pressure tests before anyone cuts concrete. It also means a trench dug to reach a pipe can fill with groundwater, so we plan the work with pumping and clean-up in mind.

Warm spots on the floor

A patch of tile or wood that feels warm underfoot, especially when the heater is not in use, often means a hot water line is leaking beneath it. The escaping hot water heats the concrete above it. You may also notice the water heater running more often, or your hot water bill rising with no change in habits.

Water coming up through the floor right now

Water under a slab looks for a way out. It travels to cracks, expansion joints and the slab edge, then wicks into carpet, bubbles under laminate, or pushes tile grout apart. If a wet area keeps returning after you dry it, or the baseboards swell from the bottom, the water source is under pressure and not a one-time spill.

Hearing water when nothing is running

A faint hiss or trickle in a quiet house, most noticeable at night, is the classic sign of pressurized water escaping. Stand on the floor in different rooms and compare. The sound is often loudest above the damaged section. We use ground microphones to narrow it to a few feet.

The meter that keeps moving

Close every fixture and look at the water meter. If the dial or leak indicator still spins, water is leaving the system somewhere. A moving meter together with any of the signs above makes a slab leak the leading suspect, and it is worth an after-hours call. For other possible sources, our leak detection service rules out toilets, irrigation and drips before anyone discusses concrete.

Deciding between a spot repair and a reroute

Spot repairs make sense when the pipe is otherwise in good shape and the leak is near an accessible area. Rerouting makes sense when the line under the slab is hard to reach or has failed more than once. We explain what each path means for your floors, your walls and your schedule.

If we find several pinhole leaks along the same line, patching one just moves the pressure to the next weak point. In that case a full repiping plan usually costs less over time than repeated slab openings, and we show you where each leak is so you can compare.

A slab repair, a reroute or a repipe all fall under the state plumbing code, Florida Building Code, Plumbing, a code that follows the IPC. Which of them needs a permit is a city question, so confirm it before the slab is opened.

What a slab leak visit looks like

Typical sequence on site

  • Confirm the leak with a meter test and by isolating the water heater and each branch line
  • Listen along the floor with acoustic equipment and mark the loudest zones
  • Scan with thermal imaging to follow a hot line's path
  • Pressure test the suspect line to confirm before cutting
  • Walk through the options, the likely floor impact and a clear price before work starts

Cutting concrete is dusty and loud, and it often uncovers flooring that was not meant to be disturbed. We cover and vacuum, and we discuss with you in advance which finishes need patching afterward. If the wet area turns out to be outside the footprint, it may be the supply line from the meter, covered under water line repair. Permit questions for repairs or repipes go to the Building Inspection Division at 904-247-6100.
