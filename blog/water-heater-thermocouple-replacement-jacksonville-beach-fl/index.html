Gas Water Heater Thermocouple Replacement: What to Check and When to Call

If the pilot on your gas water heater lights but will not stay lit once you let go of the button, a worn thermocouple is the most common cause. It is a small copper-tipped probe that sits in the pilot flame and tells the gas valve the flame is real. A replacement part is inexpensive and many homeowners can swap one safely.

One rule comes first. If you smell gas at any point, leave the house, do not touch a light switch or phone inside, and call your gas utility or 911 from outside before you call anyone else, including us.

Gas smell means leave first

A rotten-egg odor near the heater, the meter or any gas appliance is not a maintenance problem. Walk out, leave the door open behind you, keep everyone away from the house, and call the gas utility or 911 from a safe distance. Nobody should relight a pilot or test anything until the utility says the home is clear.

What the thermocouple actually does

A gas tank heater keeps a tiny standing flame called the pilot. The thermocouple is a short metal rod held in that flame, and the heat makes it generate a very small electrical signal. That signal holds the gas valve open.

If the flame goes out, the rod cools, the signal drops, and the valve closes on its own. That is the whole point of the part. It stops raw gas from flowing into the room when nothing is burning.

Over time the tip gets coated with soot or the metal fatigues and the signal weakens. The pilot then lights fine while you hold the button, but the valve drops out a few seconds after you release it.

Signs the thermocouple is the problem

Symptoms that point to the probe

  • The pilot lights while you hold the control knob down, then goes out within seconds of letting go.
  • The pilot flame looks weak, yellow or lazy instead of a steady blue flame touching the tip of the probe.
  • The heater ran fine yesterday and you have relit it more than once this week.
  • The tip of the probe looks black, bent or crusted when you shine a flashlight at it.
  • There is no gas smell and no sign of scorching around the base of the heater.

If every item on that list fits, you are in thermocouple territory. If any of them does not, keep reading, because several other parts can mimic the same behavior.

How to replace it if you are comfortable doing it

Start by turning the gas control to off and waiting several minutes so any leftover gas clears. Close the gas shutoff on the supply line to the heater. Take off the burner access panel so you can see the pilot assembly.

The thermocouple is held by one nut at the gas control valve and a small clip or bracket at the pilot. Loosen the nut by hand or with a small wrench, free the bracket, and slide the old probe out. Buy the exact replacement by the length and fitting marked on the old one or on the heater's data plate.

Seat the new probe in the same bracket so its tip sits in the pilot flame. Snug the nut at the gas valve and do not overtighten it, because the fitting is soft. Open the gas supply, then follow the lighting instructions printed on the heater label.

Before you close the panel, brush soapy water on the fitting you touched. Any bubbling means a leak, so shut the gas back off and call a licensed plumber instead of trying again.

When it is not the thermocouple

A fresh probe does not fix a heater with a different fault, and guessing wastes a weekend. The table lays out the common look-alikes and who should handle each one.

Heaters tucked into garages and closets can also lose their pilot because of drafts or because lint and dust have clogged the air path. Cleaning around the base is fine, but do not stuff anything into the vent or the burner area.

Why a new probe sometimes fails again

If the pilot drops out again a few days after a clean swap, something else is feeding the problem. A dirty pilot tube starves the flame, a loose bracket moves the tip out of the heat, and a failing gas control valve can ignore a perfectly good signal.

Sediment inside an old tank also makes the burner run longer and hotter at the bottom, which cooks components near the flame. At that stage a repair visit is worth it only if the tank itself is sound. A gas water heater repair visit tells you which path makes sense.

Try yourself

Relight per the label, clean visible dust from around the base, and replace a clearly blackened probe if you are comfortable with tools.

Call a plumber

Any gas smell after you have been cleared by the utility, a leaking fitting, soot, scorching, a pilot that will not stay lit after a new probe, or a controller that clicks without heating.

Call the gas utility or 911

A gas smell at any time. Leave the building first and make the call from outside.

Repair or replace the whole heater

A thermocouple is cheap, but it is not always the right place to spend effort. If the tank is leaking, rusted at the base or has been limping for years, the new probe only buys a short delay. Moisture around the bottom seam almost always means the tank is finished.

When the heater is old and also needs a gas valve or burner work, the repair total can approach the cost of a new unit. Ask for the clear price before work starts on both options and compare them side by side. Our team lays out the water heater replacement option next to the repair so you are not guessing.

Gas heaters and permits in Jacksonville Beach

Jacksonville Beach asks for a plumbing e-permit through its Building Inspection Division, and the application has a classification for fuel gas work and a field for whether the gas source is natural or propane. That means a simple relight or probe swap is one thing, but changing gas piping or replacing the heater may need a permit approved and posted before work starts. Ask the Building Inspection Division what applies before you open anything beyond the access panel.

The city also sits at or near sea level and has hurricane season from June 1 to November 30. After a storm, moisture, debris or a power event can disturb a heater in a garage or closet, so if a heater has been exposed to flooding, do not relight it yourself. Flood-exposed gas controls need a licensed plumber to inspect them first.

Quick safety checklist before you relight

  • Smell the area first. Any gas odor means leaving and calling the gas utility or 911 from outside.
  • Keep the area clear of paper, paint, gasoline, cleaners and anything flammable.
  • Read the lighting instructions on the label and follow them in order.
  • Never bypass or jumper a safety control to keep the pilot going.
  • Stop if the pilot still will not hold after a fresh probe and call a licensed plumber.

A gas water heater is covered by the manufacturer's standards and the Fuel Gas volume of the Florida Building Code, which is why a pilot that will not stay lit is a safety item. If you smell gas, leave and call your gas utility or 911.
