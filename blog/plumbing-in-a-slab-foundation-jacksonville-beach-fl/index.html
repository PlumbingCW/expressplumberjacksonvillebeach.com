Plumbing in a Slab Foundation: How It Works and What Goes Wrong

Many Florida homes sit on a concrete slab poured at ground level, with no crawlspace and no basement. That is cheap to build and solid, but it changes how plumbing works, because some of the pipes end up under the concrete.

This guide explains what runs under a slab, what runs overhead, which signs point to a leak and how to decide what to do.

What a slab-on-grade house is

A slab-on-grade home sits on a concrete pad poured directly on prepared ground. Before the pour, plumbers place the drain lines in the ground, and the concrete then covers them. Walls are framed on top of the slab.

Many homes in this part of Florida are built this way, although older homes and some elevated builds are different. If you are not sure what yours has, look for a crawlspace access panel or an open space under the floor. If neither exists, you likely have a slab.

What runs under the slab, and what does not

Slab-on-grade plumbing layout
LineUsual locationWhat it means if it leaks
Drain and waste linesUnder the slab, then up into wallsDamp floor, odor, slow drains, sometimes sinking or settling
Sewer lateralUnder the yard to the street connectionBackups, soggy spots in the yard
Hot and cold supplyIn walls and attic in many homes, under the slab in othersHot spots on the floor, high bill, running meter
Gas linesOften overhead or in walls, sometimes under the slabGas smell, leave the home and call the gas utility or 911
Water heater connectionsCloset or garage, above floorVisible puddles

The drains under a slab are the ones you cannot reach without breaking concrete or tunneling. Supply lines depend on the builder. Some houses run water lines overhead in the attic and drop them through walls. Others run them in the slab. Ask your plumber to tell you which yours has.

Why slab plumbing fails

Shifting soil

Ground moves as it wets and dries. A pipe held rigidly in concrete can be pulled at its joints.

Corrosion

Some pipe materials corrode where they touch concrete or soil, and the damage starts from the outside.

Abrasion

Pipe that rubs against concrete or a hard edge can wear through with expansion and contraction.

Roots

Tree roots look for water and can enter drain joints under the yard and slab.

Bad installation

Poor bedding or a bad joint at install may fail years later.

Signs of a slab leak

What to look for

  • A water meter that keeps moving when every tap in the house is off
  • A warm spot on the floor, especially with a hot water line leak
  • The sound of running water when everything is off
  • Damp carpet, lifted tile or a floor that smells musty
  • Cracks in walls or floors that appear without another cause
  • A water bill that rises with no change in use

Any one of these has other explanations. A toilet that runs, a dripping spigot or an irrigation leak can all move a meter. Rule those out first. If the meter still runs with everything off and the signs fit, a slab leak becomes likely.

Drain leaks under a slab behave differently from supply leaks. They do not raise the meter, since the water is leaving the house, not entering it. Instead you may notice a sour smell, a slow drain that no snake fixes, or a damp patch on the floor near a bathroom. A camera run through the line is the reliable way to see a crack or a separated joint.

How to test for a leak yourself

Turn off every tap, appliance and the ice maker. Find the water meter and note the dial or the small triangular flow indicator. If it moves, water is going somewhere. Then close the main valve at the house. If the meter stops, the leak is inside your property. If it still runs, the leak is before the house valve.

If the leak is inside, walk the house. Check under sinks, behind the toilet and at the water heater. If you find nothing, a hidden leak in a wall, overhead line or slab is possible. A professional leak detection visit locates it with acoustic listening, thermal imaging and pressure testing, so nobody has to guess where to cut.

Slab homes on low ground in Jacksonville Beach

Jacksonville Beach sits at or near sea level on San Pablo Island, and roughly two-thirds of the municipal area is water. That means the ground under a house is often wet or close to wet, which matters for slab plumbing. Moisture around a pipe makes corrosion and soil movement more likely, so a slow leak can go unnoticed.

The 2020 census counted 12,789 housing units in the city, with 11.2 percent vacant. A vacant or seasonal home matters for leaks, because water can run for a long time before anyone sees it. If you leave a home for a stretch, closing the main valve is a simple precaution. The city's water office at 904-247-6241 can answer questions on the city side of the meter.

The right choice depends on the pipe, the number of failures and how the house is built. Our slab leak repair team explains each option and gives a clear price before work starts. If the leak is a drain line, a camera shows where the pipe is cracked or separated.

What to do now, and when to call

If you suspect a leak, shut the main valve and call. Standing water under a slab can undermine the soil and soak flooring, and the damage gets worse with time. Insurance usually covers sudden and accidental water damage, not wear or the failed pipe itself, so photograph everything and check your policy.

If the water is coming from the yard or the line between the meter and the house, our water line repair work may apply. For homes in a neighboring town, our Neptune Beach service reaches the same way.

Pipe under a slab is installed and tested to the IPC based Florida Building Code, Plumbing before concrete is poured. That standard is the reason a slab job is inspected while the lines are still visible.
